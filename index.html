Phishing works because it looks normal. What gives it away is one small detail.

Spot the fake

Two sender addresses. One is real. Tap the fake one.

Not just email

Email phishing
Mass messages posing as banks, couriers or software vendors.
Spear phishing
Written for one person, with their name, role and real projects.
Smishing and vishing
Texts and phone calls: “parcel on hold”, “your bank calling”.
Business email compromise
A fake boss or supplier asks for urgent money or gift cards.
Quishing
A QR code that leads to a fake login page.

Six red flags

The address is off
Read what follows the @. Lookalike domains are the oldest trick.
The link goes elsewhere
Hover, or long-press on a phone, before you click.
Urgency or fear
“Within 24 hours.” “Account suspended.” Pressure kills checking.
It asks for something sensitive
Passwords, codes, card numbers, payment outside the usual process.
Generic or odd wording
“Dear user”, strange phrasing, logos slightly wrong.
Unexpected attachment
Invoices, HTML files, “enable content” prompts.

Six levers

Social engineering targets people, not software. These are the levers attackers pull.

Urgency

“Your account closes in one hour.” Pressure removes time to verify.

Authority

A message “from the CEO” or “IT” makes people comply without asking.

Fear

“Suspicious login detected.” Panic makes the link feel like the safe option.

Reward

Refunds, prizes and bonuses invite you to hand over details.

Curiosity

“Is this you in the photo?” The only way to find out is a click.

Familiarity

A reply inside a real thread, or a colleague’s hijacked account.

Real cases

It happens to large, well-run companies too.

2011

RSA

Staff received emails titled “2011 Recruitment plan” with an Excel attachment. One opened it, and the malware led to a breach of the company behind SecurID tokens. Treat unexpected attachments as suspicious.

2015

Google and Facebook

A scammer posed as a real hardware supplier and sent fake invoices from 2013 to 2015. Both companies paid, and the total passed 100 million dollars. Confirm payment changes on a second channel.

2020

Twitter

Attackers phoned employees posing as internal IT and walked them to a fake login page, then took over high-profile accounts. Call the real helpdesk back on a known number.

If you clicked

  1. Disconnect from the network if you opened a file.
  2. Change the password you entered, and any account that shares it.
  3. Report it to IT right away. Speed matters more than embarrassment.
  4. Check for new forwarding rules and unknown sign-ins.
  5. Keep the email as evidence. Do not delete it.

Stay safe

  • Go to the website yourself instead of using the link.
  • Turn on multi-factor authentication, ideally an app or a security key.
  • Use a password manager. It will not fill a fake domain.
  • Verify money or data requests on a second channel.
  • When unsure, ask. A short delay beats a breach.
